#### Interface Definitions

I wrote all seventeen interfaces, numbered M1 to M4 for mechanical, E1 to E8 for electrical, and I1 to I5 for information and software, using the block names from our functional block diagram. These are the main decisions in them:

- **M1 (Housing and feeder).** Padded clips on the corners of the feeder's 7 × 7 in tray hold the housing underneath it. Nothing is drilled or replaced (TS.06), nothing is glued, the housing stays clear of the inscription (TS.04), and the feeder still hangs from its own ring at its current height.
- **M2 (Housing, camera, and motion sensor) and M4 (Housing, user, and weather).** The motion sensor's dome passes through a gasketed hole under a hood, because a clear window over it would block the infrared it senses. Printed mounts fix the camera's angle, so the classifier sees the same view after every refill. The kill switch and status LED face the house, which lets Don switch the system off from the window before he lifts the feeder.
- **E1 (Battery pack and ESP32).** I specified two swappable packs. At about 200 mA, the Feather's built-in charger takes more than 12 hours to fill a 2500 mAh pack, and at our 48-hour target the system uses about 1,250 mAh a day. Charging in the housing, even with a faster charger board, would keep the feeder inside and away from the birds for hours. With two packs, Don swaps in a charged one during a normal refill and charges the other indoors, where the cells stay warm enough to charge in winter, and he never has to buy a battery (FS.09).
- **E2 (Kill switch).** The draft's diagram put the kill switch between the battery and the ESP32. I moved it to the board's enable pin, where tying EN to ground turns off the 3.3 V regulator and everything on it while the battery stays connected. The motion sensor's 5 V boost converter takes its enable signal from that 3.3 V rail, so it shuts down too, and the sensor cannot back-feed the unpowered ESP32 through its output. A pull-down holds the light off whenever the ESP32 is off, asleep, or restarting.
- **E3 (Power budget).** To last 48 hours, the system has to average under about 49 mA. The ESP32 therefore spends nearly all its time in deep sleep and keeps Wi-Fi sessions short. Wi-Fi transmission draws up to 368 mA, close to the board regulator's 500 mA peak rating, so the ESP32 shuts the camera down before it transmits. That peak is also above TS.12's 100 mA limit, and the budget says we will revisit TS.12.
- **E4 (Motion sensor and ESP32), E5 (Camera and ESP32), and E6 (ESP32 and buzzer).** Because the PIR needs at least 4.5 V, which is more than one battery cell gives, it runs from the 5 V boost converter. Its output wakes the ESP32 through an input-only pin. The draft's diagram had a "low quality webcam", but the ESP32 has no native USB for a webcam to plug into, and a parallel camera would have taken about 15 pins, nearly all the board had left. I chose an SPI camera module that buffers its own frames instead. It was a part we would have to buy, and it was my answer to Dr. Fu's comment that the kit has no camera. The buzzer plays a PWM tone set in software, starting at 25 kHz, and because the kit buzzer has no published frequency rating, an ultrasonic transducer is the fallback.
- **I1 (Wake and capture).** Motion starts image capture but never turns on the deterrents by itself. The motion wake is off during the day, since birds would keep waking the board. Capture runs every 10 seconds, adjustable up to 30, to reconcile the block diagram's 10 with the concept selection's roughly 30.
- **I3 (ESP32 and external processor) and I4 (Result to deterrence).** The classifier returns the same fields (frame number, label, confidence, and model version) whether it runs on the ESP32 or on a laptop over Wi-Fi. The deterrence logic is then the same either way, and the camera, the classifier, and the deterrents can be built before we decide where classification runs. A result more than 2 seconds old counts as unclear, so the deterrents never fire on a stale image; we had discussed that delay risk at the concept review. I4 also defines the "Boolean for state determination" that the block diagram drew into deterrence without explaining. It is true only when the clock has been set since power-up, it is night (sunset to sunrise, with margins Don can adjust), the label is deer, the confidence meets a threshold our camera trials will set, and the result is fresh.
- **I5 (Settings and event log).** Since Don likes to adjust his equipment, the system serves a settings page for a few minutes after it is switched on. It also logs every decision with its frame number, so each one can be checked against its image.
