#### Interface Definitions

The section was empty apart from the assignment prompt. I defined seventeen interfaces, four mechanical, eight electrical, and five for information and software, named after the blocks in our functional diagram so the two read together. The assignment describes interfaces as the contracts that let subsystems be developed in parallel, and most of my decisions came from making them work that way.

- **Attaching without changing the feeder.** Padded clips hook the corners of the feeder's 7 × 7 in tray and hold the housing underneath it. Nothing is drilled or glued (TS.06), the housing stays clear of the glass panels and the inscription (TS.04), the rest of the rim stays open for perching, and the feeder still hangs from its own ring at its current height.
- **Sealing and mounting.** The motion sensor's dome sits in a gasketed hole under a hood rather than behind a window, because a clear cover would block the infrared it senses. The camera looks out a window at the feeding edge on a printed mount that fixes its angle, so the view the classifier learned doesn't shift between refills. The buzzer has its own drained pocket sealed off from the electronics, the light points down and is shaded from Don's window and the neighbors' houses, and the kill switch and status LED face the house so he can switch the system off from the window before lifting the feeder.
- **Two swappable battery packs.** The Feather's built-in charger runs at about 200 mA, so a 2500 mAh pack takes more than 12 hours to fill, while the 48-hour target uses about 1,250 mAh a day. A few minutes on the charger during a refill can't make that up. A faster charger board in the housing would still keep the feeder inside for hours with no birds feeding, and the Feather's own charger would keep it inside for twelve hours or more every other day. Swapping in a charged pack at refill keeps the feeder inside only as long as a normal refill, charging indoors keeps the cells within their 0 to 45 °C charging range in winter, and Don never has to buy a battery, which keeps the intent of FS.09.
- **The power budget and TS.12.** Forty-eight hours on the pack's 2375 mAh minimum means averaging under about 49 mA, so the ESP32 spends nearly all its time in deep sleep and keeps Wi-Fi sessions short. Wi-Fi transmission peaks at about 368 mA, close to the board regulator's 500 mA peak rating, so the ESP32 shuts the camera down before it transmits. That peak is also far above the 100 mA limit in TS.12, so I stated that TS.12 can't hold as written and needs to be revisited.
- **A safe off state.** The draft drew the kill switch between the battery and the ESP32. I connected it from the board's enable pin to ground instead, which shuts off the regulator and everything it powers without disconnecting the battery. The 5 V boost converter for the motion sensor takes its enable signal from that same rail, so it shuts off too and the sensor can't feed power back into the unpowered ESP32 through its output, and a pull-down keeps the light off whenever the ESP32 is off, asleep, or restarting.
- **The sensors, camera, and buzzer.** The motion sensor needs at least 4.5 V, more than one battery cell gives, which is why the design has the boost converter. Its output goes to an input-only pin that can wake the ESP32 from deep sleep, and the ESP32 ignores it for the minute it takes to settle after power-up. The draft's "low quality webcam" couldn't be a USB webcam at the feeder, because the ESP32 has no native USB, and a parallel camera would take about 15 of the board's remaining pins, so I specified a camera module with its own frame buffer on the SPI bus, as a part to buy. That answered Dr. Fu's comment that our kit has no camera. The buzzer plays a PWM tone set in software, starting at 25 kHz, and because the kit buzzer has no published frequency rating, an ultrasonic transducer on the same signal is the fallback.
- **One result format.** Whether classification runs on the ESP32 or on a laptop over Wi-Fi, it returns the same fields: the frame number, a label, a confidence, and the model version. The deterrence logic doesn't change when processing moves, so the camera, classifier, and deterrence work can go ahead in parallel while that choice stays open. A result more than 2 seconds old counts as unclear, so the system never acts on an old image, which was the delay risk we discussed at the concept review.
- **What the Boolean means.** The block diagram drew a "Boolean for state determination" from image processing into deterrence without defining it. I defined it as true only when the clock has been set since power-up, it is night (sunset to sunrise, with margins Don can adjust), the label is deer, the confidence clears a threshold our camera trials will set, and the result is fresh. Motion only starts image capture and never fires the deterrents by itself, which answers Don's point about motion. The motion wake is off during the day, because birds would wake the board all day, and without a valid time the deterrents stay off. Capture runs every 10 seconds, adjustable up to 30, which reconciles the block diagram's 10 seconds with the roughly 30 in our concept selection.
- **Settings and a log.** For a few minutes after the system is switched back on, it serves a settings page where Don can change the target animals, the confidence threshold, the capture interval, the night margins, and the tone and flash settings, and every decision is logged with its frame number, so each result traces back to the image behind it.
